04 A Field Guide to Tactics

A tactic is a short sequence of moves that uses a concrete threat to gain material, improve a position, or deliver checkmate. Many tactics rely on forcing the opponent to respond before they can carry out their own plan.

Forcing Moves

1. The Fork

A single piece attacks two or more targets at the same time.

Why it works: The opponent usually has time to save only one target, leaving another vulnerable. Knights and pawns often deliver surprising forks.

2. The Pin

An attacking piece threatens a target that cannot move without exposing a more valuable piece behind it.

Absolute pin: The king is behind the pinned piece, so moving it is illegal. Relative pin: A valuable piece, such as the queen, is behind it; moving is legal but may lose material.

3. The Skewer

Sometimes called a reversed pin, a skewer attacks a valuable piece with a less valuable target behind it.

Why it works: The valuable piece must move out of danger, exposing the piece behind it to capture.

Creating and Exploiting Open Lines

4. Discovered Attack

A piece moves out of the way and reveals an attack by a piece behind it.

Why it works: The moving piece can create a separate threat while the newly uncovered piece attacks its target.

5. Discovered Check

A discovered attack that opens a line of attack directly onto the opposing king.

Why it works: The opponent must answer the check immediately, giving the moving piece time to create or carry out another threat.

6. Double Check

Both the moving piece and the piece it uncovers give check at the same time.

Why it works: The king is checked along two lines, so blocking or capturing one attacker cannot stop both. The king must move.

Manipulation Tactics

7. Deflection

A move forces a defending piece away from a square or duty it needs to protect.

Why it works: A threat or sacrifice distracts the defender and leaves an important target unprotected.

8. Decoy

A move lures an opposing piece onto a square where it becomes vulnerable.

Why it works: Drawing a piece onto a specific square can set up a fork, a pin, or a mating attack.

9. Interference

A piece is placed between a defending piece and the target it protects.

Why it works: Breaking the defensive line disrupts coordination and can leave the target exposed.

10. Overloading

A defender is responsible for protecting more than one important target.

Why it works: Attacking one target forces the defender to choose which duty to abandon.

Structural and Timing Tactics

11. Clearance Sacrifice

A piece is sacrificed to clear a square, file, or diagonal for another piece.

Why it works: Removing your own blocker can open the path for a decisive attack.

12. The Windmill (Seesaw)

A rare combination of repeated discovered checks and other checks, often making the king move back and forth.

Why it works: The repeated checks create time to win material while the opposing king is forced to respond.

13. Zwischenzug (In-Between Move)

An unexpected forcing move is inserted before an expected reply, such as recapturing a piece.

Why it works: A check or stronger threat changes the order of the exchange and can improve its final outcome.
