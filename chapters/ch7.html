07

Converting Your Advantage

The endgame often begins when queens or many pieces have been exchanged, but there is no single material count that defines every endgame. The key change is that the king can often leave shelter and join the action.

01

Activate the King and Create Passed Pawns

  • Bring your king toward the center and toward weaknesses. A passive king may leave pawns undefended or let the opponent's king take over.
  • A passed pawn has no opposing pawn ahead of it on its file or adjacent files that can stop it by capture. It can force enemy pieces to spend time blockading it.
  • Push a passed pawn when it is safe and supported, but do not advance it blindly into a blockade or tactical loss.
  • Improve your king and pieces, restrict the opponent, and only then choose a pawn break when the position calls for it.
02

Use Your Rooks Actively

Tarrasch's practical rule is to place the rook behind a passed pawn when the position allows it—behind your own pawn to support it, or behind an enemy pawn to attack it along its path.

Rook activity, king safety, and the pawn's promotion threats all matter. Do not follow a rule mechanically if a check, tactic, or more urgent task takes priority.

03

Understand Opposition and Key Squares

  • Opposition: when kings face each other with one square between them, the side not to move may be able to make the other king give way. The exact result depends on the pawn position and available routes.
  • Key squares: in a pawn ending, squares that let the attacking king support the pawn's advance can determine whether promotion is possible. Their location depends on the pawn and king arrangement.
  • Square rule: for a lone pawn with no king support, imagine a square from its current square to its promotion square. If the defending king can reach that square in time, it may catch the pawn; always account for whose turn it is and any obstacles.
04

Know the Main Rook-Endgame Plans

  • Lucena position (winning method): in the classic setup, the attacking king supports a seventh-rank pawn while the defending king is cut off. The attacker uses the rook to build a bridge against checks and help the king shelter the pawn's promotion.
  • Philidor position (drawing method): in a common defensive setup, the defender uses the king to block the pawn and the rook to check from the side or rear at the right moment. Exact placement and timing are essential.
05

Match the Minor Piece to the Position

  • Opposite-colored bishops can increase drawing chances because each bishop controls squares the other cannot; the position and number of pawns still matter.
  • A knight can be strong against a bishop when it has a stable central outpost and the opponent's pawns are fixed on squares the bishop cannot attack easily.
  • Bishops often gain value when play exists on both sides of the board and their diagonals are open.
Before pushing a pawn

A four-step safety check

  1. Can the pawn be stopped or captured immediately?
  2. Can your king support its advance or reach a key square?
  3. Can a rook get behind the pawn or cut off the opposing king?
  4. Does the move allow a check, stalemate, or tactical resource?

Study tip: set up Lucena and Philidor positions on a board and practice both sides. Knowing the goal is useful; accurate piece placement is what makes the method work.
